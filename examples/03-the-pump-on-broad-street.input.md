# The Pump on Broad Street

In late August 1854, people living around Broad Street in the Soho district of London began dying of cholera, and they died quickly. A victim could feel well in the morning and be buried by the weekend. Within a week, more than five hundred residents of the neighbourhood were dead. Nobody knew what caused cholera. The dominant theory of the day held that the disease travelled through the air, carried by foul smells rising from the city's filth. That theory was called miasma.

John Snow was a London physician who had spent years studying cholera, and he did not believe in miasma. He had noticed, in earlier outbreaks, that cholera seemed to follow the water supply rather than the air. So when the Soho outbreak began, he started walking the streets with a map.

On the map he marked every death with a short black bar at the address where the person had died. When enough bars accumulated, a pattern emerged that the numbers alone had not shown: the deaths clustered around a single point, the water pump on Broad Street. Some blocks near the pump had lost almost every household. Because Snow drew the deaths as marks on a map instead of writing them as a list, the shape of the outbreak became visible at a glance.

The map also carried two facts that complicated the story, and Snow used both of them. First, a nearby brewery had lost almost none of its workers. The brewery had its own well, and its employees were given beer as part of their wages, so they drank little water from the pump. Second, a workhouse a few streets away had suffered few deaths, and it too had its own water source. These were not exceptions that weakened the theory; they were places that had opted out of the exposure the theory predicted mattered.

Snow took his findings to the local authorities and argued that the pump handle should be removed. The handle was taken off on 8 September. The outbreak was already fading by then — the epidemic had largely burned through the susceptible population — which is why some historians argue that removing the handle did not itself stop the deaths. Even so, the removal is remembered because it marked the moment a public authority acted on the waterborne theory.

What Snow had built was not a proof. He could not yet point to the organism that caused cholera; that would come decades later. What he had was a method: plot the cases by location, look for the shape, and then test the shape against the places that should not fit. When he later obtained the records of the water companies, he used the same logic on a much larger scale, comparing cholera deaths between districts served by two different companies drawing from different stretches of the Thames. Districts supplied by the contaminated intake had far higher death rates. That comparison turned a neighbourhood pattern into an argument about cause.

The lesson people take from Broad Street is usually about data visualization, and the map earns that reputation. But the more transferable part is the move that came before the drawing and after it: Snow had a hypothesis, he looked for an arrangement of the evidence that would reveal whether the hypothesis was right, and he deliberately went looking for the cases that disagreed with him.
